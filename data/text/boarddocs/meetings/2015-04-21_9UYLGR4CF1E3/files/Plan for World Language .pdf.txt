                       Tri-State Report Essential Questions and Feedback


Essential Question #1
To what extent is the World Language 6-12 curriculum effectively sequenced and articulated
within and across languages?

     At present, there is little evidence of a documented cohesive curriculum that spirals
      vertically throughout the levels of the same language, or horizontally across languages As
      a result, the Tri-State visit team suggests that the department embrace the district
      initiative of Rubicon Atlas Maps and move toward developing common curriculum maps
      to align practice and establish rigorous, common benchmarks by language level.
     In working to establish a common curriculum, the team suggests that maps be aligned to
      meet the NYS Standards of Checkpoints A, B, and C as well as ACTFL’s Five C’s of language
      education and consider offering level-by-level maps for Spanish, French and Latin.
     Further, the team suggests that the department collaborate to design authentic
      performance based assessments that address specific benchmarks at the end of each unit
      to ensure effective sequencing and articulation.
     When designing an aligned curriculum, the department might consider smoothing the
      transition between eighth and ninth grade. The visit team identified several areas that
      require revision and refinement to diminish the inconsistencies between the middle and
      high school language acquisition experience. Expectations for spelling and diacritical
      marks, integrated use of technology, consistent use of the target language, as well as a
      review of current instructional practices are components that need consideration when
      developing the 6-12 shared vision and curricular goals.
     A major factor impeding the development of a shared curriculum is departmental
      collaborative preparation time. The visit team discussed the possibility of using monthly
      departmental meeting time; superintendent conference days, summer work, or, if
      possible, release time for this purpose.

Essential Question #2
To what extent does the World Language program balance age appropriate skill development,
the acquisition of content, and higher level thinking?
     The question of age appropriate skill development coupled with the acquisition of content
      and higher level thinking led to considerable discussion within the Tri-State team. We
      raised this question: Which specific critical thinking skills are students able to perform at
      the beginning, intermediate and advanced language levels while still maintaining the
      consistent use of the target language? In order for the World Language Department to
      answer this, it is important to first address the question of age appropriate skill
      development and higher level thinking, separately.
     As discussed in the response to the first essential question, the World Language program
      will benefit from the development of an articulated grades 6-12 curriculum through
      Rubicon Atlas. Within this process, the department can generate grade level proficiency
      standards or benchmarks that couple skill development and the acquisition of content at
      the various levels.
     The team agreed that the clarity of expectations for language students is a critical element

      as students move through the program. Clarity reduces redundancies, helps to maintain
      the rigor required of language students, supports teacher use of the target language, and
      encourages students as they experience the complex intellectual processes linked to
      learning a language.
     It is suggested that the World Language department collaborate to define the term “rigor”
      through scheduled discussions about age appropriate skills and higher order thinking. For
      example, higher order critical thinking skills...conceptualizing, applying, analyzing,
      synthesizing... create authentic opportunities to demonstrate learning and can be
      integrated into the grades 6- 8 curriculum.
     Once defined and agreed upon, the critical thinking skills need to be articulated to
      students and parents, and the curriculum and learning experiences need to be assessed
      for rigor.
     Finally, the question of differentiation is an important one when considering the balance
      of appropriate skill development, the acquisition of language, and higher order thinking.
      The team did not see evidence to indicate that teachers embed differentiation in daily
      practice, nor did we hear a common definition or understanding of differentiation as we
      spoke to staff.
     Therefore, it is important to mention that the visit team noticed that the degree of
      difficulty between the Regents and the honors level classes is significant, not just in the
      pace and scope of content, but also in the use of performance-based assessments, as well
      the amount of time that instruction occurs in the target language. We recognize that the
      standards are higher in honors level classes, but we suggest that some of the more
      preferred practices that are occurring regularly in honors classes can also be integrated
      into Regents level classes in order to ensure a rigorous language experience for all
      students in the program.

Essential Question #3
To what extent does our program support the development of a set of skills and/or
dispositions (risk taker, effective communicator, and flexible thinker) that encourages
students to use the language in advanced or authentic experiences?
   It is clear to the visiting team that the district values the development of a set of skills and
      dispositions that enable students to be successful 21st Century learners. The district’s first
      stated theory of action and goal identifies the following 21st Century skills and
      dispositions: “problem solver, flexible thinker, collaborative learner, effective
      communicator, empathetic citizen, and self-reliant, reflective, creative risk-taker.” In turn,
      the 2008 World Language mission statement refers to offering students an authentic
      language-learning experience. The sense of the visit team is that the World
      Language department consider revisiting and refining its mission statement to reflect the
      level of synchronicity between its vision and the district’s first theory of action outlined by
      Dr. Kor.
     Another area for the Irvington World Language Department to consider exploring is the
      use of the target language in classes across levels and languages. Perhaps one of the most
      significant ways to increase authenticity for students is to have classes taught completely -
      or almost completely - in the target language. The team suggests that the consistent
      integration of the target language be included in the department’s definition of

   authenticity; it can also be addressed in the text of the World Language Mission
   Statement.
  As the team explored this essential question, it became clear to us that it would benefit
   the department, as well as the district, to identify and share current instructional practices
   and assessments that are considered “authentic”, real life application and the incremental
   transfer of knowledge. It is important for all of the World Language teachers to agree
   upon a common definition of authenticity through these steps: decide what it is, what it
   looks like in the World Language classroom today, how it is connected to becoming a
   citizen of the world, and how it is linked to relevance.
 The integration of technology into curriculum, instruction and assessment is a powerful
   tool in developing students’ 21st Century skills and dispositions. Although the reality of
   finances and existing infrastructures cannot be denied, it is worth exploring the enhanced
   use of technology to support the work in developing these targeted skill sets.
 Finally, the visit team sees that the World Language Department is poised to link its
   curriculum development work with the integration of 21st Century skills. We reiterate that
   collaborative and well-developed performance assessments and meaningful, open- ended
   essential questions will provide opportunities for greater access and integration of higher-
   order thinking skills as well as opportunities for students to use the language in advanced
   and authentic settings.
 It would be productive to bring the department together to develop a common definition
   and understanding of performance-based assessments: how they are structured, how
   they are scored, and how student progress is monitored.

                           World Language Department Work


After reviewing the report with the WL teachers we developed next steps which
included identifying expert educators who will work with us to review our current
curriculum and guide the process of articulation and curriculum revision. To meet this
demand we identified the need to first work on department goals for teaching and
learning on a daily basis. In other words, “What do we want our classrooms to look
like?” and “What classroom practices will lead to what we want students to know and
be able to do?” In order to address the broader questions, we will work with Kathy
Mason. Kathy is a highly experienced, skilled consultant who has worked with Irvington
teachers and administrators in the past. Her work in supporting the development of
leadership skills has been her most recent focus, but as a former WL teacher we also felt
that she had much to contribute in facilitating the next steps for the WL department,
especially the work related to the larger goals and expectations for change across the
program. Below is the work the department developed after working with Kathy Mason
for a half-day full department meeting; this work was done in preparation for the
subsequent department work that will be done throughout the spring with our second
WL consultant, Greg Duncan. Greg Duncan is a former WL teacher who has been
supporting language teachers in the development of curriculum; including performance
based assessments, as well as vertical articulation.

The department will begin the work with the following dispositions as a starting point:

Effective Communicator:
An effective communicator demonstrates an ability to determine a purpose and organize their
thoughts to meet the needs of a particular audience. They use verbal, non-verbal, and written
language to clearly articulate their ideas using relevant vocabulary at appropriate skill level in
the target language. Additionally, they possess effective listening and questioning skills.

Problem Solver:
A problem solver is one who identifies a problem through a process of steps. They identify,
evaluate and break the problem down into worktable parts in order to create a plan. Problem
solvers are effective listeners who articulate their thoughts and use inferential questioning
when developing solutions. Active reflection on evaluation of and reconfiguring steps to
solutions allows students to persevere and achieve success when solving problems.

To what extent do students demonstrate the three dispositions of effective

Greg Duncan’s visit in February laid the foundation for April and May.
Over the course of four days we will begin to articulate through assessment
development what we students to know and be able to do.

Each language will work with Greg over two days. The level of work to be done is broad
and will require summer work, as well as curriculum development throughout 2015-
2016 school year.

Our initial impression of Greg Duncan was positive. His knowledge is deep. He has
worked on many levels including the development of state and local standards and
benchmarks. He also has a clear vision of a modern language classroom and what is
necessary for the development of a well-articulated language program; one that is
steeped in an expectation of high levels of learning and performance. His initial
observations were on target, he recognizes the work we need to do, while
understanding the complexities of working with the teachers and providing the
professional learning to supported changes that can and will be sustained and built on
over time.
