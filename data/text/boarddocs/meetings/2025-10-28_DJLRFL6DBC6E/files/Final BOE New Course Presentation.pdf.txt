                      WHY EARN THE SEAL?
1.   Formal recognition that a student has demonstrated the civic knowledge, skills, mindsets
     and experiences necessary to become an actively engaged member of their
     community

2.   Shows student’s understanding of, commitment to, participatory government, civic
     responsibility, and values
3.   Provides universities and colleges with a method to recognize and provide credit for
     attainment of higher level of understanding and skills in Social Studies
4.   Demonstrated to universities, colleges and future employers that students have earned
     recognition for they civic participation
5.   Recognizes the value of civic engagement and scholarship to school communities and
     society at large

                       Portrait of a Graduate
COURAGEOUS LEARNER:
The Civic Seal requires students to: demonstrate civic knowledge and civic participation, which
means researching real-world issues, evaluating sources, and applying learning to authentic civic
contexts. These experiences ask students to think critically, take intellectual risks, and learn from
mistakes as they engage in inquiry and problem solving.
ENGAGED CITIZEN:
This is the most direct overlap. The Seal explicitly promotes active citizenship, community
involvement, and an understanding of diverse perspectives. Through service-learning, civic action
projects, or participation in government, students demonstrate their ability to contribute to their
communities and to appreciate diversity and inclusion.
EFFECTIVE COMMUNICATOR:
Civic readiness is impossible without communication. Students earning the Seal must articulate
informed opinions, present civic projects, and often collaborate with peers or community partners.
These experiences directly build the skills of an effective communicator.
SELF-AWARE INDIVIDUAL:
The reflection component of the Civic Seal encourages students to think deeply about their values,
identity, and role in a democracy. Through civic action and reflection, students gain insight into their
strengths, passions, and responsibilities as individuals within society.
The NYS Seal of Civic Readiness and IHS Portrait of a Graduate are mutually reinforcing authentic
civic context, emerging as informed, empathetic, and self-directed contributors to their
communities.

                 Summary of
               Course ELECTIVE
●   Semester-long, multidisciplinary approach to world mythology
●   Explores connections between ancient cultures and modern society
●   Examines mythologies from Egypt, Mesopotamia, Greece, Rome, Northern
    Europe, Asia, the Americas, and the Paciﬁc Islands
●   Focuses on types of myths, key characters, and shared story structures
●   Includes research-based projects on additional mythologies
●   Analyzes myths as both literary and historical works
●   Traces how myths shaped legends, fables, and fairy tales

     ESSENTIAL
     QUESTIONS
● Where do we come from?
● What is it to be human?
● How do we ﬁt into the scheme of the world?
● What do different cultures say about the origin
  of our physical universe?
● What do different myths teach us about cultural
  norms and customs about different societies?

     Portrait of a Graduate
Courageous Learner
This course invites students to explore mythologies from diverse world
cultures, encouraging student curiosity about unfamiliar ideas and
traditions.

Engaged Citizen
By studying myths from around the world and identifying common
themes across cultures, this course fosters a deeper understanding and
appreciation of cultural diversity while helping students recognize the
universal human experiences that unite societies.

    Portrait of a Graduate
Effective Communicator
Students convey their analyses and interpretations of myths through
writing, discussion, and presentation, strengthening their ability to
communicate complex ideas with clarity in both spoken and written
forms.

Self-Aware Individual
Myths often explore universal human themes, encouraging students to
reﬂect on their own beliefs and experiences. Students can pursue
independent research on mythologies that resonate with their interests
or heritage, connecting academic learning to personal identity.

    An inquiry-based course that was developed by the College
                         Board to foster
●    critical thinking

●    expository & argumentative writing skills

                               Why AP Seminar?

As the foundational course of the AP Capstone designation, AP Seminar gives students a
college-based research experience. It

 ●   Encourages intellectual curiosity.
 ●   Prepares students for rigorous college-level research.
 ●   Elevates student voice & choice.
 ●   Fosters deeper understanding of real world problems, issues, and perspectives.
 ●   Promotes authentic, project-based learning.

                 What students do:

●   Explore diverse texts and media from multiple perspectives.
●   Develop research questions and use scholarly sources to study
    real-world issues.
●   Write a research report and create a group multimedia presentation
    proposing solutions.
●   Conduct independent research on a topic of personal interest.
●   Craft and defend a written, research-based argument through a
    formal presentation.
●   Complete a written exam demonstrating analysis, evaluation, and
    synthesis skills.
