                                               Scope of Services

  For years, the Collaborative for Academic, Social, and Emotional Learning (CASEL) has emphasized the
  significant role of districts in the sustainability of school change and improvement. As research evidence
  continues to surface about social and emotional learning (SEL) as a promising approach to enhancing
  academic learning, some district leaders are adopting an SEL focus as part of their plans to improve
  student outcomes.

  The primary goal of CASEL’s support to the district will be to provide district leaders with an assessment
  of their current SEL resources, needs, and capacity to plan and systemically implement SEL throughout
  the district. The process can serve as an important source of objective information and analysis that will
  ideally inform leaders’ decision making about how best to move forward with SEL in their district.

I.    Services

      A. CASEL’s staff will review and analyze district data.

      B. Two CASEL team members who specialize in districtwide SEL implementation will conduct a 1-
          day site visit to the district that will consist of:
          1. Interviews with Superintendent/CEO, board members, district leaders, and others; and
          2. Interviews with all four district principals.
          3. Small group meetings with teachers.
          4. Small group meetings with students.

      C. Based on district data and data collected during the site visit, CASEL Practice staff will develop
          and provide a report to the District’s Superintendent, which will include:
          1. An overview of the district Context for SEL
          2. Existing Strengths
          3. Potential Challenges
          4. Short and long-term recommendations

      D. Virtual review of report with superintendent (as well as other key districts leaders as indicated
          by the superintendent)

                                              CASEL Overview
                  The Collaborative for Academic, Social, and Emotional Learning (CASEL)
                   Facilitating Systemic Social and Emotional Learning Implementation

CASEL Overview
The Collaborative for Academic, Social, and Emotional Learning (CASEL), an independent not-for-profit
organization, is a leading resource for advancing the practice of social and emotional learning (SEL).
CASEL’s mission is to establish SEL as an essential part of education from preschool through high school.
The organization’s main priorities and activities are (1) to advance scientific research on SEL, (2) to expand
evidence-based SEL practice, and (3) to build the SEL field.

Since its founding in 1994, CASEL has been at the forefront of defining the SEL field and setting rigorous
standards for SEL programming and research. CASEL is widely recognized not just for the high quality of
its work but for its ability to foster boundary-spanning, collaborative relationships among leaders in a wide
range of fields. CASEL’s work is grounded in identifying ways that SEL contributes to school improvement
through both rigorous research and the experience of leading SEL practitioners. As research evidence
continues to surface about social and emotional learning as a promising approach to enhancing academic
learning,1 some district leaders are adopting an SEL focus as part of their plans to improve student
outcomes.2

Social and Emotional Learning (SEL)
Social and emotional learning (SEL) involves the processes through which children, adolescents, and adults
acquire and effectively apply the knowledge, attitudes, and skills necessary to understand and manage
emotions, set and achieve positive goals, feel and show empathy for others, establish and maintain
positive relationships, and make responsible decisions. CASEL has identified five interrelated clusters of
social and emotional learning competencies: self-awareness, self-management, social awareness,
relationship skills, and responsible decision-making. The ability to use these competencies when dealing
with daily situations and challenges provides a foundation for better adjustment and school performance
as reflected in more positive social behaviors, fewer conduct problems, less emotional distress, and
improved grades and test scores.3

The five social and emotional learning core competencies involve the development of specific knowledge,
skills, and dispositions:
    Self-awareness: The ability to recognize one’s feelings and thoughts and their influence on behaviors.
    This includes accurately assessing one’s strengths and challenges and possessing a well-grounded
    sense of confidence and optimism.

These competencies can be developed through two basic approaches: (1) systematically teaching,
modeling, and reinforcing social and emotional skills through evidence-based SEL programs integrated
into the school’s core curriculum, and (2) establishing caring, inclusive, participatory school cultures that
are supported by strong home-school-community partnerships. These strategies are mutually reinforcing.
Schools where students are becoming more self-aware, self-disciplined, caring, cooperative, and
responsible are fertile places for learning. Supportive learning environments provide numerous
opportunities for students to use and refine social and emotional competencies.

Systemic SEL?4
In Fall 2010, CASEL launched the Collaborating Districts Initiative (CDI), a unique, multi-site initiative in
collaboration with NoVo Foundation, the American Institutes for Research (AIR), and eight large school
districts (Anchorage, Austin, Chicago, Cleveland, Nashville, Oakland, Sacramento, and Washoe County).
The CDI seeks to achieve two goals: (1) build systemic support for SEL in each collaborating district,
thereby improving social, emotional, and academic outcomes for students; and (2) develop a clearer
understanding of how district leaders can effectively build systemic support for SEL, and how CASEL and
other external partners can assist them. As the only initiative of its kind, the CDI is generating tremendous
knowledge about district-level SEL implementation in order to help inform future efforts to build and
sustain systemic support for SEL in other districts across the country.

CASEL defines systemic support for SEL as the extent to which support for SEL is embedded in the
regulations, routines, resources, and culture of an educational system, where
    ●    Regulations are the formal rules and policies that can either encourage or deter educators from
         adopting practices that promote SEL.
    ●    Routines are the organizational structures and procedures that shape how work gets done (e.g.,
         structure of the central office, the process for sharing information with schools, and patterns of
         communication among departments).

    ●    The availability and allocation of financial, human (e.g., people, their knowledge, and skills), and
         social (e.g., the existence and quality of relationships among members) resources influence what
         happens in schools and classrooms.
    ●    Culture refers to the norms, values, assumptions, and beliefs that are shared among members of
         a particular community (i.e., district or school).

CASEL’s use of systemic support for SEL refers to the extent to which educational systems are designed to
support and sustain students’ social and emotional development. Focusing primarily on school districts
through the CDI, we are interested in the ways in which district regulations, routines, resources, and
culture can be altered to strengthen schools and classrooms to support academic, social, and emotional
learning for all students.

Scholars in SEL and their colleagues in other areas of education have noted that school districts are
particularly influential in determining the success and sustainability of their efforts.5 For years,
Collaborative for Academic, Social, and Emotional Learning (CASEL) has emphasized the significant role of
districts in the sustainability of school change and improvement. Districts are increasingly determining
priorities for schools and deciding how professional learning time and school budgets should be used.
Therefore, it is important that key district decision-makers find ways to support the adoption and
implementation of SEL practices throughout the district. This includes highlighting the importance of SEL
by allocating human and financial resources to it and supporting school leaders and staff in implementing
SEL given the other demands on their time and attention.6
