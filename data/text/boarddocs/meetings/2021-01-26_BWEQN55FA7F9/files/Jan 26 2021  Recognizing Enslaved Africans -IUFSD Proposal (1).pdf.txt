                          Commemorating Enslaved Africans in Irvington

  Hannah, Betty, Dick, David, Teem, Brebay, David, Bill, Jack, Bette, Bet, Caesar, Hannah, and Susanna

This memorial pays tribute to the enslaved African men, women, and children who lived and toiled in what
eventually became the village of Irvington. We honor their legacy, and reaffirm their humanity, which was
stripped from them in their time.

New York State was the capital of American slavery for more than two centuries. In the late 18 th century,
one out of every five households in Greenburgh enslaved people. Some of Irvington’s founding families
enslaved from four to ten human beings. The varied and strenuous labor of enslaved men, women and
children included clearing land, cultivating crops, threshing wheat, planting and harvesting orchards,
tending livestock, building structures and carrying out domestic work.

While these fourteen names emerge in archival records, the personal identities of Irvington’s enslaved
Africans have been lost forever, appearing as mere numbers in census records, wills, and estate
inventories. In an act of grave historical omission, it is only those who enslaved human beings who
populate our local history books and for whom many of the streets branching off our Main Street are
named.

This memorial stands in recognition of the atrocity of human enslavement. The growth and wealth of the
village of Irvington was inextricably tied to the forced labor of enslaved Africans. These people deserve to
be recognized, discussed, and remembered. May this reckoning help heal the wounds of the past and sow
the seeds for everlasting peace and brotherhood and sisterhood for all of the people of our village, our
nation, and the world.

New York's Comptex History OF SLAVERY

From New York's beginning as a Dutch colony, enslaved people were considered chattel, property
to be traded and sold like cattle and wheat. Just before the English took over in 1664, the Dutch
West India Company granted conditional or half-freedom to select enslaved men and their wives,
stipulating dues and unpaid labor obligations. Their children (even the unborn) remained
enslaved. The British then replaced the Dutch system with harsher slave laws. After independence,
in 1799, New York passed a Gradual Emancipation Act, followed by a second one in 1817 (with
myriad modifications in between). Historian Vivienne L. Kruger explains its impact: “In contrast to i
sudden, total emancipation in the South, New York slaves were freed voluntarily and gradually
between 1785 and 1848. Separate ownership guaranteed separate manumission of relatives and
severe family disruption as husbands, wives, and children were freed individually, often many
years apart.” As such, children born into slavery after July 4, 1799, would be freed, but only after
long service: 25 years for women and 28 for men. People born before this date were renamed
“indentured servants,” but essentially remained enslaved since owners could be reasonably
confident these newly “free” people would stay by their enslaved children. Slavery officially
ended in New York in 1827, but the children remained in bondage until their period of servitude
"expired. Kruger concludes, “it was not until the mid-1870s . . . that an entire generation began to be |
| bom ..., whose parents had not been enslaved or ‘bound youth’ in New York State.” |
