Science Instruction at Dows Lane K-3
                 New York State Science Standards




Our Amplify Science program, “blends hands-on investigations, literacy-rich activities,
and interactive digital tools to empower students to think, read, write, and argue like real
scientists and engineers.” Amplify Science

In Kindergarten, our students begin the year with a unit about the needs of plants and
animals. Students learn about the systems of plants and animals that live together in a
habitat and consider how to bring monarch caterpillars back to a community garden. In
the winter, students learn about matter and temperature. And, in the spring, students
develop and use models to learn about the effect of sunlight (energy) on Earth’s surface
(matter). Students also gather weather data for interpretation.

In 1st grade, students start the year with a unit about animal and plant defenses.
Students investigate how plants and animals survive using structures such as spikes,
armor, and camouflage. Students use their knowledge to explain how an aquarium sea
turtle will be able to survive once she is released back into the ocean. Later in the year,
students learn about the spinning Earth. They find patterns in data to figure out what
causes nighttime and daytime, and explore the changing position of the sun in the sky.

In 2nd grade, students begin the year with a unit about plant and animal relationships.
They plan and conduct investigations to figure out what plants need to grow and ways
that plants rely on many animals to disperse their seeds. The students end the year with
a unit about changing landforms. Students take on the role of geologists as they use
models to figure out how small-scale changes to landforms can add up to large-scale
changes over long periods of time.

In 3rd grade, students begin the year learning about classification and then delve
deeper into inheritance and traits. Students learn about patterns in traits between
parents and offspring, relatedness, and the influence of environment on traits by
studying wolves. During the winter, students play the role of biomimicry engineers
studying a population of snails. They think about the systems made of organisms and
the environment in which organisms live to understand how the environment affects
organisms' likelihood of survival. In the spring, students take on the role of
meteorologists as they study weather and climate patterns. They use patterns from
weather and climate data to make scientific arguments about where to establish an
orangutan reserve.
