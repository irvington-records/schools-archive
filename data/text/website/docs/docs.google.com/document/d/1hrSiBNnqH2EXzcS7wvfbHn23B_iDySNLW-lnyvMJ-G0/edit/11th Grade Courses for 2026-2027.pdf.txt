Dear Parents/Students:

The Irvington High School Course Catalog is a comprehensive document outlining and
explaining all of our course offerings. This document is a tremendous tool for students and
families and can be used to help align students towards their post-secondary goals. By
considering courses throughout your entire high school experience, you will be better prepared to
make strong selections for next year. I encourage you to keep in mind the following guidelines
as you make your selections:

       student’s transcript and ask two questions. The first one is obvious – how well did
       the student do in their courses? But the second one is equally important – did this

       had to offer?

       requirements. There are various requirements for different classes, and it’s
       important for the student and their parents to know those requirements.

       will be made to provide Requested Courses; in some cases changes may be required.

                           ENGLISH: 11th GRADE FULL-YEAR COURSES

AP English Language & Composition*
(full year, 1 credit, Honors course open to juniors and seniors)
The AP English Language and Composition course aligns to an introductory college-level
rhetoric study and writing curriculum, which requires students to develop evidence-based
analytic and argumentative essays that proceed through several drafts. Students evaluate,
synthesize, and cite research to support their arguments. Throughout the course, students develop
a personal style by making appropriate grammatical choices. Additionally, students read and
analyze the rhetorical elements and their effects in non-fiction texts, including graphic images as
forms of text, from many disciplines and historical periods. It is strongly recommended that
students have a minimum of a B average in English if they are considering taking the course.
Students should be able to read and comprehend college-level texts.

Students who take this class are expected to sit for the AP exam in May.

AP English Literature and Composition*
(full year, 1 credit, Honors course open to juniors and seniors)
This Advanced Placement English course emphasizes the careful reading and critical analysis of
literature, 1600 to the present. Essays and in‐class writing are frequent, and research is a critical
component of the course. AP Literature is open enrollment but an assessment will be given at the
beginning of the year so students can familiarize themselves with the demands of the class. It is
strongly recommended that students have a minimum of a B average in English if they are
considering taking the course. Students should be able to read and comprehend college-level
texts.

Students who take this class are expected to sit for the AP exam in May.

AP Seminar (Anticipated for the 2025-2026 School Year)
(full year, 1 credit, Honors course open to juniors)
Step into the world of discovery with AP Seminar, a dynamic course designed to sharpen your
research skills and spark meaningful conversations about the complex questions shaping our world.
Together, students will dive into diverse and engaging materials which range from
thought-provoking literary classics to cutting-edge news articles, personal stories, scholarly studies,
and evocative media.
In this course, you won’t just learn, you’ll explore, question, and create. You'll develop your own
viewpoints and engage in scholarly research practices to support them. You’ll bring your ideas to life
through compelling essays, presentations, and team projects. Whether you're working solo or
collaborating with peers, you'll practice the art of critical thinking and hone effective oral and written
communication skills that will set you apart in college and beyond.

AP Seminar isn’t just about learning facts; it’s about mastering how to think, argue, and lead with
confidence.


This course can be part of a two year AP sequence which culminates in AP Research.

Students who take this class are expected to sit for the AP exam in May.


Reading America: Conversations and Controversies*
(full year, 1 credit)
This course is an exploration of the texts that attempt to capture the complexity that is America.
There is not a singular American experience or identity. Rather, it is a radical experiment that,
by definition, is ever-changing and is understood through a multitude of lenses, including tribal
nations predating European colonization, the historic compromises of the founders, and the
modern lens of the inhabitants. The work of this class will involve responsible and reflective
reading, writing, presenting, speaking, and listening as a collective intellectual community to
engage in the social, political, and economic conversations about America, past and present.
Through the use of diverse canonical and modern authors we will explore how different voices
contribute to our understanding of America. We will consider questions such as Which voices
are heard? and Which voices are overlooked? Who narrates the stories that we read? How does
a shift in narrative shift our own perspectives?

Media Studies
(Every day, 1 credit )
For elective credit only
Students will learn and practice journalistic writing and production skills in every facet of newspaper
production. The course will provide instruction and hands‐on experience in reporting and writing, editing,
layout and production, as well as the opportunity to be published in the student‐operated newspaper, The
Paw Print.

Analysis of Visual Literature*
(½ year, ½ credit, open to juniors and seniors)
This course is a rigorous survey of film genres, theory, and history. Students will analyze films as visual
text, consider how filmmakers use the medium in order to convey messages, and study the evolution of
film as a genre. The course will examine the evolution and development of film.Reading assignments
will include various critical theory companion pieces, critical film reviews. And works of fiction that
correspond in theme or genre. Students will write extensively in order to analyze films as visual text and
interpret them through the lenses of various literary theories.




Existential Literature: Heroes & Victims*
(½ year, ½ credit, open to juniors and seniors)
From Shakespeare’s Hamlet to television’s Eleanor Shellstrop, existential characters struggle between
living meaningful, authentic lives in a disordered, chaotic world or surrendering to the numbing absurdity
of modern life. In this course, students examine how existential thinkers and writers confront this
dilemma by creating both existential heroes and victims. Students will analyze representative texts from a
variety of authors, genres and time periods, including Jean Paul Sartre, Rainer Maria Rilke, Kate Chopin,
Philip K. Dick, Kurt Vonnegut, David Foster Wallace, Margaret Atwood, and others. In addition to works
of print literature, this course will analyze examples of existentialism in film and television as well.


Graphic Novels & Comic Books as Literature*​
(½ year, ½ credit, open to juniors and seniors)
This course examines graphic novels and comics as a literary art form, analyzing works in a variety of
genres. From the re-imagining of Joseph Campbell’s “hero’s journey” in popular comic book superheroes
past and present to the socio-cultural impact of graphic novels such as Persepolis and Maus, this course
seeks to strengthen students’ ability to decipher the signs and symbols of text, to build a knowledge of
different modes of literary criticism and to apply various critical lenses to the engaging world of
sequential art. Class website: tinyurl.com/ihscomics


Journalism*
(½ year, ½ credit, open to juniors and seniors)
Through the news itself and related nonfiction texts, students will study the First Amendment, journalism
ethics, the future of print journalism, and new media. The first half of the course emphasizes the use of
critical thinking skills to understand journalism and content through multiple perspectives. The second
half of the course has students complete an inquiry based research paper, following the protocols of the
Modern Language Association, to develop and prove a student generated thesis about a current trend,
issue, or subject in journalism.

Advanced Creative Writing*
(½ year, ½ credit, open to juniors for elective credit only*)
Designed for students who have a genuine interest in creating original written work, this course will allow
students to experiment with advanced literary techniques in the areas of character and plot development,
word choice, style, and structure. In a workshop setting, students will incorporate these techniques as they
develop a writing portfolio that includes both finished and in-progress pieces, some of which can be
submitted for publication. Students will read works by established authors which will serve as mentor
texts, and practice exercises will reinforce skills and help students master techniques. Ongoing peer
review and revision are also major components of the course. Special attention will be given to narrative
nonfiction, with an emphasis on skills and techniques useful in composing the college essay.

SUPA Public Speaking*
(½ year, ½ credit, Honors course offered through Syracuse University, open to juniors for elective
credit only*)
Speaking in public is an essential component of most professions as well as a necessary skill of active
citizens, who must be able to articulate, advocate and argue, in public, about public issues. Hence, the
instruction of presentational speaking is based on two important principles: the need to understand the
fundamental principles of rhetoric and the need to practice different types of speeches. Both objectives are
directed toward developing workable presentational skills, understanding the link between topic and
audience, learning the process of crafting speeches, strategically incorporating digital media, lending
support to major claims and implementing persuasive strategies to enhance audience engagement. It is
strongly recommended that students have a minimum of a B average in English if they are considering
taking the course. Last year’s tuition cost was approximately $110 per credit; students may apply for
financial assistance from Syracuse University. If a student is admitted to the course and chooses to enroll,
he or she must officially register for the course with Syracuse University, which will result in a financial
commitment to the University. Upon successful completion of the course, enrolled students will earnn
three college credits from Syracuse University (accepted for “core” or elective credit at over 90% of
universities).

United States History (full year, 1 credit)*
As per the New York State Framework for Social Studies, this course begins with the colonial and
constitutional foundations of the United States and explores the government structure and functions
written in the Constitution. The development of the nation and the political, social, and economic factors
that led to the challenges our nation faced in the Civil War are addressed. Industrialization, urbanization,
and the accompanying problems are examined, along with America’s emergence as a world power, the
two world wars of the 20th century, and the Cold War. Students explore the expansion of the federal
government, the threat of terrorism, and the place of the United States in an increasingly globalized and
interconnected world. The course concludes with the United States History & Government Regents
Examination.

AP United States History (full year, 1 credit)*
Prerequisite: Global II students wishing to take AP US History should be carrying a B average in that
course at the time when course selections are made.
The American History Advanced Placement course is offered to juniors who are highly motivated and
prepared for a rigorous course.It is highly recommended, due to the high degree of cognitive tasks in
APUSH, that students seeking to enroll in APUSH maintain at least a B+ throughout their Global II
course. The curriculum is designed to introduce students to the concepts and critical events in American
history, which would be introduced to college underclassmen in a survey course. Students will do
supplementary reading in order that they will prepare themselves to take the Advanced Placement Test in
May. AP students must also take the Regents Examination in American History at the conclusion of the
course.

                              SOCIAL STUDIES SELECTIVES

AP Psychology (1 year, 1 credit)*
The AP Psychology course is designed to introduce students to the systematic and scientific study of
behavior and mental processes of human beings and other animals. Students are exposed to the
psychological facts, principles and phenomena associated with each of the major sub‐fields within
psychology. They also learn about the ethics and methods psychologists use in their science and practice
(the full course description is available at apcentral.collegeboard.com).

AP World History (full year, 1 credit)*
Prerequisite: Students should have a minimum of a 90 in Global I Honors and a 92 if in Global I
The course highlights the nature of changes in global frameworks and their causes and consequences, as
well as comparisons among major societies, beginning with the foundations of civilization and continuing
to the present. It emphasizes relevant factual knowledge, leading interpretive issues, and skills in
analyzing types of historical evidence. Periodization, explicitly discussed, forms an organizing principle
to address change and continuity throughout the course. A writing sample will be required of candidates
prior to admission to this honors course. In addition, students in this course must take the New York State
Regents Examination in Global History and Geography. Juniors must get special permission from the
instructor.


Sociology (½ year, ½ credit)*
This one semester elective course involves a study of human society. Issues such as gender, race, poverty,
class, crime, and deviance are discussed. Students will read from scholarly books, articles, and essays, as
well as study some of the above issues through videos and discussions. Several projects, research essays,
and presentations will be required. This course is open to seniors. Juniors must get special permission
from the instructor.

Psychology (½ year, ½ credit)*
This course will explore the ways that psychological knowledge can be seen and applied in our everyday
lives. In addressing the question of "Why do people do what they do?," it will offer a survey of research
and approaches across the major areas of psychology, including learning, motivation, cognition, memory,
perception, emotion, personality and social psychology.

Current History and Politics (1950’s to the Present) (½ year, ½ credit)*
This course gives the student the opportunity to explore political, economic and social issues that have
occurred within the last half of the 20th century and the first part of the 21st century. Although the focus
will be on the United States, there will be considerable attention to world events. Students will be
expected to use and respond to a variety of sources. Participation will be a key requirement. Students will
also be expected to have knowledge of the daily news.This course is open to seniors, juniors and selected
sophomores.

AP European History (full year, 1 credit and open to seniors)*
Prerequisite: It is strongly recommended that students have a minimum of a B average in all previous
social studies classes if they are considering taking the course
This course offers a study of European History from 1450 to the present, taking a broad perspective on the
shaping of the modern world. Themes include the nation‐state and political ideologies, capitalism,
science and technology, the individual in artistic expression and democratic movements, and Europe’s
position in world history. In‐depth studies of representative topics (such as Florence under the Medici, the
Dutch Golden Age, Galileo and the Scientific Revolution, the French Revolution and Napoleon, Nazism
and the Holocaust, the Berlin Wall, and the European Union) help to explore the broader themes. As an
Advanced Placement course, it promotes academic rigor equivalent to college introductory courses,
including supplemental readings, independent initiative, and preparation for the AP European History
exam in May. Students will develop their skills at analyzing historical evidence and interpretations,
assessing multiple perspectives, expressing historical understanding in writing and other media, and
discussing current events in light of historical examples. Juniors must get special permission from the
instructor.

AP Research (1 year, 1 Credit)
AP Research is an interdisciplinary course that encourages students to demonstrate critical
thinking and academic research skills on a topic of the student’s choosing. Students must have
successfully completed the AP Seminar course in order to enroll in AP Research.

Sports History and Marketing (½ year, ½ credit; 1 year, 1 credit)
This course includes a dual focus on the history of sports and sports marketing. There will be a review
and historical perspective on sports and sports culture in the United States. Students will study these
facets via the media, readings and research activities. In addition, students will gain a basic introduction
of marketing concepts. Marketing concepts and theories that apply to sports will be developed and
exercised by the students. Sponsorship, management, marketing plans, and promotion will be covered in
this course. This course will also delve into the application of this knowledge in marketing strategies. This
course is open to seniors, juniors and selected sophomores.

Prerequisites: Students will be required to fulfill entrance criteria for Honors selection.
Mandatory summer work must be completed by any student wishing to take the course by the first

Highly motivated students are expected to use skills such as critical thinking, developing and using

Regents Chemistry (full year, 1 credit) (Lab)*
Prerequisites: Students must have taken and passed the Living Environment course & Regents
exam and should have taken or be enrolled in Geometry. It is also recommended, but not required
that students have taken Earth Science.
Students will develop skills such as critical thinking, developing and using models, interpreting and

with a focus on relationships, processes, mechanisms and applications. Topics include matter and change,
atomic structure, chemical bonding, chemical reactions, and behavior of solutions. Students will take the
New York State Physical Setting Chemistry Regents Exam provided they have fulfilled the 1200 minute
lab requirement.

Honors Chemistry (full year, 1 credit) (Lab)*
Prerequisites: Students must have taken and passed the Living Environment course & Regents
exam and should have taken or be enrolled in Geometry, and fulfilled the entrance criteria for
Honors selection. It is also recommended, but not required that students have taken Earth Science.

Highly motivated students are expected to use skills such as critical thinking, developing and using
models, interpreting and analyzing data and constructing scientific explanations to study the major
concepts of matter and the changes that matter undergoes, atomic structure, chemical bonding, chemical
reactions, and behavior of solutions. This course is a faced paced, rigorous, and mathematically oriented
study of chemistry with technology infused throughout the curriculum. Students will take the New York
State Physical Setting Chemistry Regents Exam provided they have fulfilled the 1200 minute lab
requirement.

 A classical view of physics with major emphasis placed on fundamental concepts. This Lab oriented

Prerequisite: Students must have taken and passed Biology as well as the LE exam and should have

This lab‐oriented course is designed to prepare physical science and engineering students for college
level thinking in physics and engineering. Topics include mechanics, electricity, magnetism, energy,
waves & light. Successful students will benefit from a high level of personal responsibility as they
accomplish classroom tasks, complete projects/labs, outside readings and independently solve all

AP Biology (full year, 1 credit) (Lab)*
Prerequisite: Final grade of an 83 or higher in Biology & Chemistry.
The AP Biology course is designed to be the equivalent of a two‐semester college introductory biology
course usually taken by science majors during their first year. The AP Biology course is designed to be
taken by students after the successful completion of a first course in high school biology and one in high
school chemistry, as well. It aims to provide students with the conceptual framework, factual knowledge,
and analytical skills necessary to deal critically with the rapidly changing science of biology. Successful
students will benefit from a high level of personal responsibility as they accomplish classroom tasks,
complete projects/labs, outside readings and independently solve all necessary problems. All students are
expected to take the College Board’s AP Exam at the end of the year.

AP Chemistry (full year, 1 credit) (Lab)*
Prerequisite: Final grade of an 83 or higher in Biology & Chemistry.
The AP Chemistry course is designed to be the equivalent of the general chemistry course usually taken
during the first college year. This college level course will examine topics such as the structure of matter,
kinetic theory of gasses, chemical kinetics, chemical equilibrium, acid‐base theory, and thermodynamics.
The course contributes to the development of students’ abilities to think clearly and express their ideas,
orally and in writing, with clarity and logic. Successful students will benefit from a high level of personal
responsibility as they accomplish classroom tasks, complete projects/labs, outside readings and
independently solve all necessary problems. All students are expected to take the College Board’s AP
exam at the end of the school year.
AP Environmental Science (full year, 1 credit) (Lab)*
Prerequisite: Final grade of 83 or higher in Biology and a physical science (Earth Science or
Chemistry)
This course places an emphasis on the scientific principles and methods required to understand the
interrelationships of the natural world, the identification of environmental problems, the evaluation of
risks and the discovery of alternate solutions. Assignments in this course begin to move away from basic
information recall and shift to the application of the information on new situations. Field trips will be
complemented with laboratory experiences to further build upon the content that will be covered in class.
Successful students will benefit from a high level of personal responsibility as they accomplish classroom
tasks, complete projects/labs, outside readings and independently solve all necessary problems. All
students are expected to take the College Board’s AP exam at the end of the school year.

AP Physics 1 (full year, 1 credit) (Lab)*
Prerequisite: Final grade of an 83 or higher in Biology, Chemistry and Algebra 2.
The AP Physics course is an introductory first semester college level laboratory course equivalent. The
AP Physics class develops the students’ abilities to read, understand and interpret physical information;
describe and explain the sequence of steps in the analysis of a particular physical phenomenon or
problem; use advanced mathematical reasoning including algebra, and trigonometry; perform and create
experiments and interpret the results of experimental uncertainties through a lab oriented classroom in
which students need to be self-directed. Successful students will benefit from a high level of personal
responsibility as they accomplish classroom tasks, complete projects/labs, outside readings and
independently solve all necessary problems. All students are expected to take the College Board’s AP
exam at the end of the year

Honors Forensic Science (full year, 1 credit)*
Prerequisite: Final grade of an 83 or higher in Biology & Chemistry.
This course is focused upon the application of scientific methods and techniques to crime and law. This
course is intended to provide an introduction to understanding the science behind crime detection.
Scientific methods specifically relevant to crime detection and analysis will be presented with emphasis
placed upon the techniques used in evaluating physical evidence. Topics included are blood analysis,
organic and inorganic evidence analysis, microscopic investigations, anthropology, hair analysis, DNA,
drug chemistry and toxicology, fiber comparisons, paints, glass compositions and fragmentation,
fingerprints, soil comparisons, and arson investigations, among others. Laboratory exercises will include

Latin 1 (full year, 1 credit)*
Latin 1 is a course which promotes literacy and cultural awareness. In addition to learning the
fundamentals of the Latin language, the student will be introduced to the Roman way of life,
Greco-Roman mythology and Roman history. Students develop an ability to handle the language in four
skill areas: listening, speaking, reading and writing. This course provides a firm foundation for the study
of modern foreign languages. In addition, students will gain a greater understanding of the English
language through the study of derivatives and English grammar as it relates to Latin.

Latin 2 (full year, 1 credit*
Prerequisite: Successful completion of Latin 1.
After a thorough review of first year grammar, students will continue their study of Latin grammar,
culture and mythology, while further developing the four skills areas of listening, speaking, reading and
writing through the study of Latin syntax and literary style. Students will compare and contrast ancient
cultures with their own culture and learn the ideals that guided men and women in the past.

Latin 3 Honors (full year, 1 credit)*
Prerequisite: Successful completion of Latin 2
Students will review Latin grammar by reading the Roman versions of Jason and the Argonauts, The
Odyssey and The Story of Hercules. In the second half of the year, as students learn the more
sophisticated grammatical concepts of Latin, the focus will move to reading authentic Latin prose,
including selections from Caesar, Livy, Sallust, Cicero, and Ovid. There will also be discussions of
Roman mythology, history and politics, especially the role of Julius Caesar at the end of the Roman
Republic. The course will culminate with a locally-developed Latin Regents Exam.

Ancient Greek I H - An Introduction to Ancient Greek (full year, 1 credit)*
This course will present the students with an introduction to the Ancient Greek alphabet and language,
with an emphasis on English derivatives, Greek culture and readings of Greek texts. A special emphasis
is placed on learning about the Ancient World through reading authentic passages of Ancient Greek as
well as through English readings giving an overview of Ancient Greek Civilization. This course aims to
give students an appreciation not only for the richness of the Ancient Greek language, but also the culture
and civilization of the Ancient Greeks and their tremendous impact on Western Civilization.

Ancient Greek II H- Ancient Greek Prose (full year, 1 credit)
Prerequisite: Successful completion of Greek I H
This course is a continuation of the topics and themes covered in Ancient Greek I- An Introduction to
Ancient Greek. After a brief review of the grammar and vocabulary of Greek I, students will continue
their study of the Ancient Greek language and culture. The centerpiece of the course will be learning
about the culture, history and mythology of the Ancient Athenians through the reading of real passages of
Greek authors such as Plato, Herodotus, Apollodorus, and Demosthenes. Through this students will gain
a richer understanding of how sophisticated and beautiful Greek culture and literature is, and be able to
compare the society and literary styles of the past with the greatest works of Western Civilization.

Ancient Greek III H- Ancient Greek Philosophy, Rhetoric and History (full year, 1 credit)
Prerequisite: Successful completion of Greek II H
This course will introduce students to the authentic writings of Plato, Lysias, Xenophon and Herodotus.
Students will spend the first semester reading selections from Plato’s Republic, as an introduction to
Greek philosophical arguments and rhetorical style, while expanding their vocabulary and grammatical
understanding of Ancient Greek. In the second semester, students will choose which author they want to
pursue and will read extensively in their selected author.

World Mythology (half year, 1/2 credit)
This course introduces students to a multidisciplinary approach to world mythology, providing students
with an overview of mythology and its relationship to ancient cultures and culture today. In this course,
students will explore the mythology of the cultures and religions of Egypt, Mesopotamia, Greece, Rome,
Northern Europe, Asia, the Americas and Pacific Islands. This course covers the purposes and types of
myths; development of myths and mythological characters and the common elements of mythological
structures. Literary elements such as symbols, themes, and plots of myths are analyzed, enabling the
student to identify common characteristics and patterns in myths originating in various cultures and
ancient religions throughout human history. Students will explore other mythologies through
research-based projects, analyze these stories as works of literature and of history, and examine how these
stories of mythology have helped to develop other forms of literature, such as legends, fables, and fairy
tales. (This course will be taught in English)

Architectural technology is a collaboration of courses offered in design and drawing with emphasis on
modern technology such as CAD (computer aided drawing) and scale model building, entwined with
Project Lead the Way (a program for engineering design and S.T.E.M. education), the successful

Design and Drawing for Production (full year, 1 credit)
*Satisfies New York State Requirement for Art.
Drawing & Design for Production is a foundation course on the high school architecture pathway. This
course exposes students to some of the authentic concepts and techniques used in drafting and
architectural design. Starting with the design process, students will develop style and technique through a
series of design problems that will introduce a variety of materials, working methods and tools. Students
will engage in the complete design process, conceptualize their ideas through technical drawings,
maintain a design journal, and apply mathematical concepts in the design and building of their pieces.
Students will work with a balance of hand drafted drawings, CAD modeling, and architectural and
structural modeling projects.

Students will learn basic drafting ranging from orthographic projection, multiview drawings, isometric
and axonometric perspective, and residential construction details. Students will explore a variety of
materials and techniques and learn the appropriate and safe techniques to alter these materials using hand
tools. They will learn to choose the best technique and materials to create ideas they envision. An
incorporation of basic design knowledge, spatial visualization and modeling projects will give the student
an excellent base for more advanced technology study.

Project Lead the Way is pre‐engineering curricula that help schools prepare a new generation of

Introduction to Engineering Design (full year, 1 credit)
*Satisfies New York State Requirement for Art
Introduction to Engineering Design (IED) is a high school level foundation course in the PLTW
Engineering Program. In IED students are introduced to the engineering profession and a common
approach to the solution of engineering problems, an engineering design process. Utilizing the activity
project-problem-based (APB) teaching and learning pedagogy, students will progress from completing
structured activities to solving open-ended projects and problems that require them to develop planning,
documentation, communication, and other professional skills. Students are introduced to the engineering
design process, applying math, science, and engineering standards to identify and design solutions to a
variety of real problems. They work both individually and in collaborative teams to develop and
document design solutions using engineering notebooks and 3D modeling software.

Through both individual and collaborative team activities, projects, and problems, students will solve
problems as they practice common engineering design and development protocols such as project
management and peer review. Students will develop skill in technical representation and documentation
of design solutions according to accepted technical standards, and they will use current 3D design and
modeling software to represent and communicate solutions. In addition the development of computational
methods that are commonly used in engineering problem solving, including statistical analysis and
mathematical modeling, are emphasized. Ethical issues related to professional practice and product
development are also presented.

Principles of Engineering (POE) is a foundation course of the high school engineering pathway. This
survey course exposes students to some of the major concepts that they will encounter in a postsecondary

The course applies and concurrently develops secondary level knowledge and skills in mathematics,

Physical Education
Upon commencement from Irvington High School, all students must earn two credits over four years of
Physical Education to satisfy the New York State mandated requirement. Physical Education emphasizes
health-related fitness and developing the skills and habits necessary for a lifetime of activity. These
courses provide students with opportunities to achieve and maintain a health-enhancing level of physical

application of rules and strategies of complex difficulty in the following different movement forms:

flexibility), aerobic exercise, team sports, individual and dual sports, Adventure Education, and
recreational games. Ongoing assessment includes both written and performance-based skill evaluations.

    scheduling conflicts.
