Abridged version of
Scream Machines
by Laura T. Johnson
1 Roller coasters. They thrill and terrify us at the same time. Even their names

are intended to scare us. Whether you're a roller coaster fan or not, you have to
agree that these “scream machines” are amazing. Science makes roller coasters
pretty easy to understand. Before a ride begins, the cars are standing still because
no force is acting on them. Then an engine and chain pull the cars up the first hill,
called the lift hill. The lift hill is always the tallest. The cars begin to move up the
track because a force is at work pulling them. When the cars slide down the other
side, another force is at work. That force is gravity. Gravity pulls all objects down
toward the center of the earth. After the lift hill, the cars go up and down the other
hills on their own.

2 G’s are a measurement of the change in the force of gravity. When a roller
coaster makes you fall faster than gravity would pull you to the ground, you
experience negative g's. It's the feeling of weightlessness that riders experience
when they are lifted up off of their seats. Positive g’s are the opposite. When roller
coasters soar through a loop, positive g's push the riders into their seats and make
them feel heavier. Normally, gravity pulls equally on all your body parts. But when
you're speeding down the hill of a roller coaster, you're going so fast that your
stomach—as well as other body parts—is not being tugged by gravity in the usual
way. Your stomach feels as if it has risen into your throat!

5 Roller coaster cars have running wheels, up-stap wheels, and side-friction
wheels. Each set of wheels has a separate position and a special purpose. Roller
coasters also have several kinds of brakes. Some brakes bring the train to a
complete stop. Others slow the

A Gift for Ma

1 The wind wrapped itself around the sod cabin. The pioneer family inside
were used to the sound. The wind seemed to be howling since they had moved
to Nebraska two years before. In the spring of 1865, they had packed up all they
owned and joined a wagon train to make the difficult trip from Philadelphia. Now
Charles Lund had 160 acres of land and more work than he could handle.

2 As young Martha Lund sat stitching a quilt, she thought about how hard her
parents worked. Her mother did the laundry on a washboard, cooked, cleaned,
and even sewed clothes to trade for goods in town. Her father was always in
the fields. Martha also worked the fields when she wasn’t helping Ma with the
household chores.

3 Martha worried about how tired Ma looked. After everyone went to bed each
night, Martha could hear her mother working away at the sewing machine. The
girl wished she could do something special for her mother. And then an idea came
to her. She had seen Ma admiring a bonnet in a shop window.

4 “Pa, my quilt is almost done,” Martha announced as she came to the dinner
table that evening.

5 Her father said, “That's a mighty fine piece of work.”

6 “Charles, I’m helping Mrs. Larsen with the new baby on Saturday. Why don’t
you two go into town so Martha can show off her quilt?” Ma suggested.

7 “That’s perfect,” Martha thought. “I'll trade the quilt for the bonnet Ma liked.
She'll be so proud to wear it to the spelling bee next week.”

& When Saturday arrived, Martha and Pa headed for the little town of

Timberville. Pa went to look at a horse he was thinking of buying. Meanwhile,
Martha headed straight for the general store with her quilt. She came out with the
bonnet for her mother. She explained her idea to her father, and he nodded. He
put his hand gently around his daughter's shoulders, and they headed for home.

9 Martha hid the bonnet until the day of the spelling bee. Then she held it
behind her back and went down to the kitchen. There Martha greeted Pa, as well
as her mather, who was smiling oddly with her hands also hidden behind her
back. Almost at the same time, Martha and her mother surprised each other with
gifts. Martha handed the bonnet to her mother, and Ma presented Martha with
a fine new dress. “I’ve been sewing nights to finish it for you,” Ma explained. “I
want you to wear it to the spelling bee.”

10 Martha laughed, “And I want you to wear the new bonnet.”

W Pa stood back, regarding them. “I guess the Lund gals will be judged to be the
prettiest—and the most generous—ladies there,” he said with his quiet smile.

Pioneer Children

i What would it be like to live without TV, video games, or computers? Now
imagine no electricity at all. Can you picture such a life? If so, you'll have some
idea of what it was like growing up 150 years ago in places like Kansas, Nebraska,
and Wyoming. :

2 Pioneers traveled westward to start a new life. By the 1840s, settlers had
reached California. But few settled on the Great Plains, the grasslands east of
the Rocky Mountains. It wasn’t until the 1860s that this area became safe for
settlement. The Homestead Act of 1862 offered 160 acres of land to anyone who
agreed to live on it, build a home, and farm it for five years. Thousands of families
found this offer too good to pass up. They piled their belongings in wagons,
hitched up horses or oxen, and made their way west.

3 Parents needed their children to help with the chores. Pioneer children were
expected to plant, weed, tend cows, and ride horses. The day for children started
at dawn. They would get water from the well, bring in wood, and lay a fire. After
that, they would milk the cows and collect eggs. Girls learned to cook, make soap
and candles, and sew. They helped do the laundry on a washboard. Without
electricity or running water, a task like this could take the whole day!

4 At around 10 years old, children started to work in the fields. Girls worked
right alongside the boys. Because children were needed in the fields, school was
only held a few months a year. Children of all ages learned from one teacher in a
one-room, log schoolhouse. Most children didn’t go past the eighth grade. By that
time, they had learned the basic skills needed to survive in their day.

5 Children also found time for fun. Toys were very costly and hard to find. So,
children would play with homemade toys or make up games. They also enjoyed
spelling bees. That's right—a spelling bee was quite an event in those days. People
came from all over to see one. Another place to have fun was at a barn raising.
Building a barn was a difficult job, so the community would get together to share
the work. Afterwards, it turned into a social event enjoyed by young and old alike.

6 Life was quite an adventure for pioneer children. It was hard work too. Do you
think you would have liked living on the frontier?
