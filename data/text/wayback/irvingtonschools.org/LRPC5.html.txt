Data Analysis
A. Demographer’s Report
One of the key decisions of the LRPC was to recommend that the District retain the services of Dr. Bruce Bothwell as District demographer. The committee believes that any set of options presented to the Board of Education and the Irvington school community must be grounded in a well-researched and defensible demographic analysis. Specifically, the LRPC believes that the Districts’ demographic analysis must reflect the increase in housing construction and the rapid turnover in home ownership resulting in the replacement of empty nesters with younger families. Dr. Bothwell, a well-respected, locally based demographer, also provides his services to many other area districts. A more intensive demographic analysis would clarify to the Board and the community both the extent of the District’s student population growth, as well as the future trends.
The demographic study was undertaken during the month of November 1998. A summary of the demographic study is available in Appendix B. The study shows that:
- The challenges presented by our rapidly growing student population will continue for the next 5 to 10 years.
- The rate of student population growth is expected to slightly decrease in Dows Lane but will very rapidly put pressure on facilities availability in the Middle School and High School. Enrollment growth from 1998-99 to 2006-07 is likely to be 37% in the Middle School (from 346 to 474 students) and 68% in the High School (from 372 to 626 students).
- Total District student population is likely to surpass the all-time District enrollment high of 1844 by 2002, but perhaps as early as 2001. This growing enrollment must be viewed in the context of different facilities utilization as determined by the changing educational environment (see Section C, page 9).
- Total District student population by the 2003-04 school year is likely to be 1937 (medium forecast) but could be as high as 2111 (high forecast) – respectively a 21% and 32% increase from our current population of 1597.
- Total District student population by the 2006-07 school year is likely to be 2029 (medium forecast) but could be as high as 2319 (high forecast) – respectively a 27% and 45% increase from our current population of 1597.
- Dows Lane is at or beyond capacity while the Middle School and High School are approaching capacity levels, as they are currently utilized.
B. Key Building Constraints
As the result of the site visits and interviews conducted with the building principals, the LRPC determined that the solution to the District’s long range space challenge did not simply revolve around how to fit more children into a school building. The solution to this challenge must be considered in the context of ensuring and enhancing the educational excellence that the Irvington community has come to expect. Below is a listing of key issues which the committee identified during its deliberations.
Dows Lane
- With approximately 900 students, Dows Lane is the largest elementary school in suburban Westchester. (There are only three elementary schools of larger size, located in Yonkers and Mount Vernon). Large schools become an anonymous environment. It becomes difficult for the Principal to properly manage the school and be familiar with all the children and parents. In addition, at this size, safety becomes a major concern.
- Activities can no longer be held for the entire school. For instance, the gym/auditorium can no longer accommodate the whole school for concerts or assemblies.
- Over 40 regular classes makes scheduling of "specials" difficult. The regular week has 40 "periods" for specials such as computer lab, music, art and gym. Of particular concern is gym which is visited twice a week by students and is divided into two: over 40 classes might mean gym less than twice a week. Also, special programs such as art may not have allocated spaces. At present, there are 40 regular classes at Dows Lane.
- Cafeteria is used continually from 11 A.M. until 1 P.M. for lunch allowing children only 20 minutes to eat. Kindergarten and 1st grade can not be accommodated in the cafeteria and must eat in their rooms.
- Location of library is not optimal. It should be centrally located and near the computer lab.
- Parking lot constraints/access to school property raise safety concerns.
- Volume and concentration of bus and vehicular traffic at dismissal time is an important safety concern.
- Storage space is limited.
- Kindergarten rooms are slightly different (bigger, proximity to bathroom). There are 7 such rooms. Kindergarten class sizes of 25 are beyond recommended levels.
Middle School
- Number of students. Maximum of about 350 as currently utilized.
- Number of science rooms is limited. In addition, each science room can only seat 24 students due to limited lab desk space.
- Problems with scheduling shared staff.
- Storage availability.
- Classrooms do not have room for computer clusters.
- Library size is small.
- Auditorium can not handle the whole school making group events problematic (maximum capacity 300).
- Parking availability/school access.
- Not enough lockers/shortage of places to put them.
- Lack of field space.
- Multi-purpose room doubles as cafeteria, band room and activity room.
- Classrooms are small by state standards.
High School
- Building capacity is approximately 350 as currently utilized.
- Will need additional science labs in approximately 2 years.
- Westwood Development access might bisect High School campus.
- Auditorium capacity is only 450.
- Art space is limited.
- General external and internal condition of the facility (opened in 1965): roof, boiler and windows will require major renovations.
C. The Changing Educational Environment
Today’s changing educational environment demands more space than ever before. The needs of education have been changing quite rapidly in recent years due to several important factors including: the adoption of full-day kindergarten, more intensive use of technology, the return of an increasing number of special education students to local districts and the state’s new academic standards. In recognition of this new reality, the state now mandates 775 square feet per classroom minimum as opposed to requiring only 550 square feet a generation ago. According to Donald McKenzie, Superintendent of Putnam-Northern Westchester BOCES, "It’s just that it’s much more complicated to provide a relevant education for children than in the old days when you could pack a lot more kids into a building." ("School Construction is Booming", The Journal News, 12/8/98 pg. 1B).
The current elementary school program of Dows Lane has a modified full-day kindergarten (half days at the beginning of the year, 2 full days added in October, culminating with 5 full days beginning in January). This program was put into place in the 1997-98 school year as the result of an extensive study undertaken by a Board of Education committee. Full-day kindergarten in Irvington is viewed as a true success and has become an integral part of our District’s elementary program. Clearly, the trend in New York State has been to enhance early education, including both pre-kindergarten and kindergarten. In fact, the New York State Legislature has enacted a "Universal Pre-Kindergarten Program" which may require districts to offer pre-kindergarten at some future time.
The proper integration of technology into the daily curriculum of our schools requires additional space. Stand alone computer labs available for instruction or group projects are a necessity at both the elementary and secondary level. This requires the use of at least one additional room per building. Furthermore, each classroom on the elementary level, according to the District’s 1998 Long Range Instructional Technology Plan will have a bank of 4 computers for student use and one for teacher use. In the secondary grades, each classroom will eventually be equipped with at least one workstation. Thus, the intensive use of technology in our children’s education will occupy a significant amount of building and classroom space.
Special education also requires more space than ever before. State and Federal law requires districts to educate children with disabilities in the "least restrictive environment". As a result, fewer children are being sent out-of-district to regional BOCES centers for education. When these students remain in-district, they require additional support services such as resource rooms and special classes. This trend is expected to continue.
Finally, the new State standards will also impact how students are taught and this too will require additional space. For example, an increased emphasis will be placed on science and technology education in earlier grades. This may require specialized facilities. In addition, every high school student must take three sciences instead of two to graduate. Science can not be studied effectively without labs that allow students to experiment. Lab space is a commodity that is in short supply at the Irvington Middle School and High School.
Changes in our High School program will also demand more space. For example, due to the tougher New York State graduation requirements, some of our High School students may need 5 years to receive a Regents Diploma, rather than the typical 4 years. This will understandably increase our student population. In addition, the High School Site Committee is currently studying the potential for requiring seniors to take 6 courses rather than the current 5. This requirement, while improving the quality of our educational program, will necessitate additional space and course offerings.
Clearly, the changing educational environment, in and of itself, has impacted the way the District utilizes its facilities. Our school buildings therefore could, in the past, accommodate a higher student enrollment than they can now and than they will in the future. Thus, as facilities needs have increased due to the changing educational environment, Irvington (as well as other districts) has also experienced a large student enrollment increase. These dual challenges will make it difficult for Irvington to meet the needs of its students with our current space constraints as we strive for educational excellence at the beginning of the next millenium.
