SOURCE http://irvingtonschools.org:80/OBMath.html | DATE 2005-02-14 | TITLE The View On Board: Math Committee

Math education is a major concern for all of us. Over the past several months, the recommendations of our Math Curriculum Committee for revising our math program have been presented at Board of Education and parent information meetings; articles on the proposal have appeared in The Enterprise and The Viewpoint; and there have been several discussions between various parents and members of the administration and Board of Education. Clearly there are "frequently asked questions" about the proposed changes to our math curriculum. This View On Board summarizes the proposed changes and responds to these questions.
First, some background: as part of the planned curriculum review cycle, our math program came under review beginning in 2002. Since then, a district committee of over 20 administrators, principals and faculty at all levels assessed and revised our K-12 curriculum. The result conforms to NYS standards that specify the skills to be taught by 4th and 8th grades, and the content areas students must master to pass the Regents exam required for graduation.
Once we had developed our curriculum, the next step was to identify am elementary program (texts, activities, hands-on components), which met our standards. Committee members visited a number of high performing neighboring districts, and identified two possibilities. During the fall, teachers piloted both programs in our classrooms, and analyzed each program on several variables. The clear recommendation from the teachers is that we adopt the Trailblazers program.
The committee also recommended a specific implementation plan. (Note that the district has already added instructional time in math in the elementary grades). As this proposed plan would change the way in which students are grouped for math, and at what grade level, there have been questions about how this will work in practice. At the present time, students are grouped into four "tracks" beginning in third grade.
So, here is the "FAQ" list:
Why is it necessary to revamp our math program; aren't the basic skills the same as they've always been?
Yes, but math has become much more language based over the years. Many of you will remember memorizing tables and formulas and applying them to numerical problems. The emphasis now is on narratives that provide the information students must use to determine the various elements of problem solving and on the mathematical operations that must be applied to specific data in order to reach a solution.
Will we still be teaching "the basics": computation, multiplication tables, operations skills?
Absolutely! NYS has enumerated 7 areas students must master: mathematical reasoning, numbers and numeration, mathematical operations, modeling/mathematical relations, measurement, uncertainty and probability, and patterns and functions/generalization. Our curriculum specifies how these skills will be taught at each grade level, and delineates expectations for student learning in each area. If the program we chose does not adequately address a component of our curriculum, we will use supplementary instructional material that does.
Why did we choose the "Trailblazers" program?
After piloting two programs in the classroom, and analyzing how well they supported our curriculum along various measures, our teachers overwhelmingly recommended "Trailblazers". This program has been in use in Scarsdale for several years, and has recently been adopted by Bedford, among other districts. We have hired a consultant, the math specialist who was responsible for the selection and implementation of the program in Scarsdale, to work with our teachers on implementing the program in Irvington.
Is it true that we will be eliminating tracking?
The Curriculum Committee recommends that, beginning in Middle School, there will be three skill level groups in math, and also proposes that tracking be eliminated in 3rd grade. Some parents feel that skill level tracking in the early grades is essential to successful math instruction. There has been considerable debate on this issue, and some disagreement about what the research in the field indicates, particularly on the benefits of tacking in the early grades. What is clear is that Irvington is the only district amongst the higher performing districts in the region that tracks beginning in 3rd grade. Therefore, the recommendation is that math instruction should be part of the regular classroom program through differentiated instruction.
What, exactly, is differentiated instruction?
It's a teaching method that we have successfully incorporated into the district's English/Language Arts (ELA) curriculum. Within the classroom context, teachers can present one lesson, with refinements to accommodate the range of achievement levels of the students.
How can they do that - effectively teach low and high achievers the same lesson?
There are two key components to a successful differentiated instruction model: a program that provides both supplementary and extension activities, and a classroom structure that accommodates small groups of children at different levels working on activities appropriate for their level.
Isn't math different from ELA? Can it be taught the same way?
Math certainly is a different set of learning and teaching tasks. However, math is based on language proficiency, and it is recommended that in Dows Lane, grades K-3 should remain with their classroom teachers for both language arts and math instruction.
Isn't this a "self-esteem" issue? Are we sacrificing the high achievers so the lower achieving kids won't feel bad?
This is not about "feeling good about yourself" in that way…as one parent put it, "I know my kid's a klutz, but that doesn't mean we shouldn't have an athletics program". However, it doesn't make sense to force young children into "tracks" when their abilities are so fluid, especially considering that students learn in different ways, and that few students perform consistently in the 7 particular skill areas. So, while our goal is not to have children feel that there is "no difference" among them, we do want to offer every student the opportunity to achieve his or her best mastery of the range of mathematical skills necessary for academic excellence.
What do the teachers think about this?
Dows Lane teachers have petitioned our Assistant Superintendent for Instruction for just this - as classroom teachers know their students best, they believe they can incorporate appropriate math instruction into their language based classroom activities.
OK, assuming that it makes sense to keep students in their home classroom through Dows Lane, and that we will begin grouping in Middle School, what about grades 4 and 5 in the Main Street School?
Whether to begin grouping in grade 4, or wait until grade 6, is a key question for the implementation of the new math program. Several considerations guided the committee's recommendation that grouping should begin in middle school:
- The new elementary math program is based on a heterogeneous classroom model, and includes instructional materials aimed at accommodating a range of student skills;
- In the Main Street School, students will now have one teacher for math/science and another for English/Social Studies. Basically, we can "track" teachers allowing them to develop differentiated instruction skills in two rather than four major curriculum areas, ensuring that all students benefit. This teaching model is incompatible with grouping students.
- Clustering within the classroom will enable students to get extra help and extra enrichment.
Can this really work for Special Ed students?
We have had great success with AIS (Academic Intervention Services) and collaborative teaching for these students. While certain students may need to be "pulled out" for small group remedial or supplementary instruction, students also benefit from a second teacher joining the regular class ("push in") to work collaboratively with the classroom teacher to provide students with extra help.
Can this really work for high achieving students?
Interestingly, the most consistent concern about our current tracking program is that our highest achieving students are not sufficiently challenged, even in Track 4. The recommended program could use a combination of enrichment activities and a collaborative teaching model, similar to that in operation for Special Ed and Track 1 students, to provide additional resources where indicated.
What about this year's 4th grade; they've been grouped for two years, and will be grouped again in Middle School?
This grade will continue in their groups, as it makes no sense to "detrack" students for one year.
As our Superintendent and Assistant Superintendent for Instruction are leaving at the end of the year, will we have sufficient administrative support for this change?
Yes. The Mathematics Department Chair (Lisa Urban) as well as the building principals and teachers at both Dows Lane and Main Street are in favor of this recommendation. The primary concern is that we provide sufficient teacher training and support, and there is a plan for this aspect of the program. In addition, our newly appointed Superintendent and Assistant Superintendent will be involved in all aspects of planning and implementing the new program.
What is the plan for preparing and supporting teachers?
The Superintendent's Conference Days on February 26 and March 25 will be devoted to training teachers in the new program. Planning for additional training this year, over the summer, and into next year is on-going. Our proposed district calendar for next year provides time for teachers to work together before the start of the school day; weekly team meetings and monthly faculty meetings will necessarily focus on implementing the math program. The planning committee includes our math consultant as well as a representative of the publisher. Note that a key element of the plan is the on-site consultant who will be available to teachers throughout the school year.
Is all of this in the budget?
The budget for the 2004-05 school year is now being developed. Funding to purchase books and materials is factored in. In addition, it is proposed that we add a teacher line to support both enrichment and remediation for students in grades 3 and 4 next year (5th grade will remain in the current program), as explained above.
What's next?
Parents will have an opportunity to comment and ask questions at the March 9 Board meeting, as the Board considers the committee's recommendations. As always, the Board welcomes your feedback. Send your emails to: board@irvingtonschools.org
