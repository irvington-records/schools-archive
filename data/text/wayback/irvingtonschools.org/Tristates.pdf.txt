Executive Summary



The Irvington Public Schools invited the Tri-State Consortium Visit Team to study its
approach to the teaching of English/Language Arts (E/LA), K-12. The Tri-State
assessment model is based on systems thinking; that is, it focuses on the degree to which
there is consistent alignment with the model’s fifteen indicators throughout the system.
The team spent three days, October 20-22, examining documents prepared by teachers
and administrators and conducting interviews with staff, parents and students. Dr.
Kathleen Matusiak, Superintendent of Schools, Dr. Ralph Napolitano, Assistant
Superintendent for Instruction, and Dr. Scott Mosenthal, High School Principal,
welcomed the Tri-State team on the first day and they were available to answer questions
and clarify evidence during our entire time in the district. They asked us to consider three
essential questions. The responses to these questions are in another section of this report,
and they are meant to assist the Irvington School District in the future planning of E/LA
instruction, K-12.

The Tri-State Visit Team spent three days examining the district’s E/LA learning
environments and opportunities. As critical friends, we came to confirm, affirm, and
recommend, using a demanding model based on fifteen indicators that are designed to
motivate an ever-improving high performing school district to do even better for its
students. We have described what we saw, identified practices already in use that have
the potential for a more systematic approach, shared our perspectives and experiences as
members of other high performing districts, asked questions to clarify our understanding
about district practices, and found data in the physical and anecdotal evidence presented
that affirm the assumptions that surfaced during the visit. In the spirit of collegiality, the
team provides feedback that is intended to help this high performing district move
educational opportunities in E/LA to another level.

The Irvington Public Schools, by every standardized measure, is a very high performing
district. Students have the opportunity to experience rich activities in their E/LA
education that require them to think critically and creatively about challenging E/LA
concepts and issues. As part of the review of the Irvington E/LA program, the visit team
was asked to respond to essential questions that related to the curriculum’s alignment and
articulation, the use of data to inform instruction and the district’s balance of expectations
for high achievement and meeting the needs of diverse learners.


Essential Question # 1: To what extent is the K-12 Language Arts curriculum
articulated and aligned?
In reviewing artifacts prepared for the visit and in interviews with staff, parents and
students, the team noted that the curriculum, written in 2002, was aligned with New York
State Standards and that students performed well on New York State assessments. In
surveys teachers reported a high degree of understanding of E/LA objectives and


                               Tri-State Consortium Irvington Visit Report   Page 4
expectations. Better than 80% of secondary teachers stated that the E/LA curriculum was
articulated and aligned.
Executive Summary (continued)


The articulation and alignment of the written curriculum was, in part, a result of the
systematic use of consultants from Teachers College who worked with staff members to
identify the criteria that were used to create the K–8 pacing calendars for reading and
writing. The calendars and interviews with elementary teachers provided evidence that
the reading curriculum is well-articulated and implemented, but that the writing skills are
not as well defined or understood. Elementary teachers reported a high degree of
understanding of strategies to teach reading skills, but a lower comfort level with writing.
There appeared to be some inconsistency in understanding how to blend Guided Reading
with Writer’s Workshop. In addition, elementary teachers identified a need to create “kid
friendly” self-assessment rubrics. Staff at the upper level have created checklists for
editing and revising, and students reported that they were familiar with these rubrics.
While the K-5 curriculum calendars define skills and strategies to be addressed monthly,
there is a need to create a more in-depth calendar for the middle school level. The team
suggests that the high school E/LA staff focus on the creation of curriculum maps,
common assessments with rubrics, and clear expectations for student achievement.
Regular meetings of the K-5 staffs have resulted in strong articulation of the E/LA
curriculum. Articulation appeared to be the result of a more informal process at the grade
6-12 level. In interviews, middle school staff members felt that grade 6-12 articulation
was “top-down” and that a process for more effective teacher input needed to be
developed. However, there is evidence of an agreed-upon sequence of content and skills
at the grade 6-12 level. The recently developed Research Curriculum responded to an
identified need for a systematic approach to the development of research skills and
provides a model for collaboration between levels.
Parent surveys indicated that better than half of the parents responding believed that there
was a clear, cohesive English Language Arts curriculum in the district. Since the survey
did not indicate whether the parents were representative of the K-12 parent population,
there is some question about whether parents of students at all levels shared this opinion.
When asked about their children’s experiences in E/LA, parents noted that “It came down
to the teacher.” This response would argue for closer supervision of classroom practices
in implementing the written curriculum. In response to questions about how well they
understood the E/LA curriculum, parents focused on the mechanics of writing. Grammar
and spelling are areas in which parents think more attention is needed. Interviews with
parents indicated a need for more grade-to-grade articulation. Some parents felt that the
focus on preparation for State assessments was excessive and left little time for more
creative activities.
The visit team agreed with the staff judgment that Irvington is well on its way toward a
Balanced Literacy approach to E/LA, but that the process would be a long one. The visit
team concluded that the district does have a process for E/LA alignment and articulation,
but it needs to implement the process. Systematic and sustained professional
development activities will be needed to institutionalize the approach.

Essential Question # 2: To what degree does our analysis of student performance
from multiple sources affect our curriculum and instruction?
From artifacts and interviews, the visit team noted that data from State assessments is
used to place students in Academic Intervention Services (AIS) classes, a pull-out
program that focuses on concepts and skills not mastered on the State assessments.
However, the team concluded that, generally, the results of these tests have minimal
effect on classroom instruction and that the data was not well articulated.
At the K-3 level, there is evidence that classroom assessments are used to pre-test
students. As a result, in many instances curriculum is compacted and instruction is
differentiated. There are examples of flexible grouping, particularly in relation to E/LA.
Literacy profiles describe student strengths and weaknesses and guide instruction. It is
the team’s recommendation that the profiles be extended beyond the primary grades.
Some teachers in grades 4 and 5 are familiar with test data, but there is limited evidence
of differentiation at these levels. While teachers indicate that data is shared at transition
meetings, additional professional development on relating data to classroom instruction is
suggested. Teachers describe an interest in analyzing multiple forms of student
assessment data; the time for these conversations should be organized and specific.
The visit team noted the need to systematize performance assessments and rubrics at the
grade 6-12 level. It is felt that these assessments, both formative and summative, could
guide teachers in modifying classroom instruction and could support the use of
differentiated instruction.
Irvington is in the beginning stages of creating ways to access data to measure the
longitudinal progress of students. While administrators have the data available, there is
no easy way to compare the progress of individual students from grade to grade, or within
a single school year. Cohort data should be used to determine trends and to measure the
effectiveness of programmatic changes.

Essential Question # 3 – To what extent to our schools balance high standards of
achievement with the educational needs of diverse learners?

The district has high and well-defined expectations for the academic achievement and for
meeting the needs of students K-12. The New York State Learning Standards in English
Language Arts are used to guide curriculum development throughout the system. The
standards were used as the basis for the development of the K-12 Language Arts
Curriculum Guide. Every E/LA teacher in the district developed this curriculum guide.
Administrators, team leaders and chairpersons have ongoing discussions with staff
related to ELA instruction. This has created an environment where staff can reflect on
goals and challenges related to student instruction.

There is a tremendous amount of data (though not longitudinal data) available to
administrators and staff. The data is utilized to determine student needs and how students

Executive Summary (continued)


K-12 performed on local and state assessments. Academic intervention services exist
across all grade levels to meet the needs of students identified by this data. Reading
teachers, especially on the elementary level, provided this service. This has had an impact
on the other services they should be providing to students. Special education teachers
serve the needs of classified students. There is a K-3 multi-sensory class at Dow’s Lane.
Collaboration exists between special education and mainstream teachers.
In addition, Special Education educators in grades K-5, have been involved in the K-5
literacy training. The K-5 is moving towards a more inclusive model. There seems to be a
need for a more collaborative model on the secondary level. In order for the needs of the
one hundred and ninety-six classified students to be addressed on a higher level, the team
recommends additional special education staffing, district-wide, especially on the
secondary level. The district should also consider reviewing the ratio of classified
students in mainstream classes.

There appears to be a need for an additional ESL teacher to meet the needs of this student
population. Instructional Service Teams (IST) exist to discuss concerns staff may have
regarding a student’s performance and needs. The IST will recommend learning
strategies for staff to utilize in the classroom. Even though the district is committed to
providing AIS required by state law, there needs to be a higher priority and more
systematic support in providing AIS services in reading and writing to students not
necessarily impacted as a result of state assessments. This would include programs to
support these needs during the summer time. In addition, the team suggests that the
literacy profile and writing portfolios be expanded to K-5.

The district offers acceleration opportunities for students on the secondary level. In the
middle school, eighth grade students can accelerate in art and Regents level mathematics
and biology. The high school offers honors or advanced placement sequences in all the
core academic subjects, including foreign language. The high school is commended for
offering such a variety of AP courses, considering its small size, and having an open door
policy regarding AP admissions. Since the open door policy invites students of diverse
needs to participate, the district should consider a more formal support system so that all
students have an equal opportunity to be successful. As far as electives are concerned, it
is recommended that the district provide more learning opportunities designed for the
lower to middle learners through additional electives and an increase in electives in
English for students across the board. We also recommend the establishment of an
alternative instruction opportunity for students.

There is a district expectation for staff to provide differentiated instruction to meet the
needs of diverse learners. The district, up to this point, has not provided the teaching staff
with the proper training to achieve this expectation. District wide professional
development is needed in this area with a focus on tiered lessons. Even though a
consultant trained the K-5 staff in balanced literacy, and the program contains a variety of
components, this type of approach cannot be considered true differentiated instruction,
especially related to tiered instruction. The team observed students working in groups on


                       Tri-State Consortium Irvington Visit Report   Page 7
Executive Summary (continued)


various levels. However, what they observed was not cooperative learning groups. The
staff should realize that placing students in groups is not cooperative learning. We
recommend the district also investigate whether the staff has been provided with this type
of training.

Finally, the structure of the learning organization can impact the extent of meeting the
needs of students. Some of the teachers on the middle school teams are shared between
grade levels. This affects the articulation involving students’ needs and the time that each
team member has to plan collaboration. We recommend a shift to a true team model
teachers being assigned to a single grade- level team. As we all know, the demands placed
on administrators have increased tremendously. In order to help ensure that the needs of
diverse learners are being addressed, the team suggests that the principal be in the
classroom as much as possible. We also recognize that being in a “testing school” and
considering the developmental stage of fourth and fifth graders, increases the pressure
and expectations of a four/five school. The team, therefore, recommends that the district
consider additional administrative staff, in terms of an assistant principal, to support the
district goals and school challenges of Main Street Elementary School.

The Irvington School District has created a warm, student-centered environment that set
the tone for the Tri-State Visit Team of critical friends. The team was immediately
impressed by the attention and thoroughness of the planning that the staff put into
preparing for the visit. The team recognizes the energy, effort, dedication and
organization of the two District Coordinators for this visit: Eileen McGuire, Assistant
Principal, Dow’s Lane School, and Scott Mosenthal, Principal, Irvington High School.
The team’s needs were consistently provided for and we express our gratitude for the
district’s generous hospitality.

                           Student Performance - Indicator #1


Educators value and utilize standards-based performance assessments in which students
demonstrate their capacity to transfer and apply knowledge (e.g. demonstrations,
exhibitions, authentic tasks, portfolios, or other assessments designed for this purpose).
These assessments will demonstrate the degree to which students integrate knowledge,
skills and higher-level thinking both within and across disciplines, as monitored over
time.

Approach:

There is a sound process in place for the systematic use of standards-based assessments
such as portfolios, exhibitions, and performance tasks, in which students are able to
demonstrate their ability to integrate knowledge, skills and higher level thinking within
disciplines

   •   The district has a plan in place in grades K-5 to assess students’ reading and
       writing abilities and to reassess on an ongoing basis, in order to inform instruction
       and to identify individual student needs.
   •   The district’s plan includes expectations that students at the middle and high
       school level will complete a variety of performance-based tasks, such as essays,
       research papers, literary analyses, and personal narratives.
   •   The district’s plan includes monthly curriculum calendars, K-12, created and
       revised, to match instruction in reading and writing.
   •   The district has a research protocol in place that requires students to complete
       authentic tasks as evidence of their ability to do research.

Implementation:

Many educators understand and regularly use a variety of standards-based performance
assessments to provide information about student learning.

   •   Students K-12 are offered varied and rich opportunities for authentic performance
       tasks that incorporate and reflect their reading and writing abilities.
   •   Middle school and high school teachers are using the research protocol to enhance
       performance assessment opportunities.
   •   At the elementary level, the curriculum calendar determines a coherent program
       of performance-based reading and writing tasks.
   •   A reading and writing portfolio of writing samples and Rigby benchmarks show
       student progress in the K-3 years.
   •   A reading profile, indicating students’ scores on yearly reading assessments, is
       being developed for each student and will be passed on from one grade to the
       next.
   •   Elementary teachers use daily observations and running records to assess their
       students regularly in reading and writing.

   •   Students work collaboratively and independently to create a new literary
       character, write a song, perform a song, and write a scene description for a play
       they are currently reading.
   •   Performance assessments in middle school are developed by teachers and used as
       a final exam. This information is transferred to the next grade level.


Results:

Some improvement in student learning related (in part) to the use of performance

   •   Student self- reflections on performance-based tasks show evidence of growth and

   •   Observational surveys and Rigby benchmarks, used at the elementary level, show

       outside of core academic subjects, such as physical education and mus ic.

       community.

   •   While the district has a high expectation for performance assessments, it might
       consider issues of articulation of approach between grades and among schools and
       consistency among assessments used in the district.
   •   Consider developing a “pure” middle school model, i.e., teams of teachers
       assigned to one grade level.
   •   Without the “pure” model in place, consider the alternative of flexible scheduling
       for middle school teams. For example, teachers across disciplines can agree to
       equitably block subject periods.

National and state norm-referenced and criterion-referenced tests provide data
regarding the students’ knowledge and higher-level thinking. This data is measured and
monitored over time, and is used to further improve student performance.

Approach:

There is a sound process in place for the selection of norm- referenced and criterion-
referenced tests and the systematic analysis of test data which measure and monitor
student achievement over time in order to improve learning.

   •   The district plan for standardized testing involves the systematic analysis and
       disaggregating of test data. This data is used to measure and monitor student
       achievement over time in order to improve learning.
   •   The district plan also includes a variety of alternative assessments designed to
       monitor student growth over time. (WRAP, Rigby PM Benchmark, Ohio Word
       Test, TONYSS etc.)

Implementation:

Many educators use test scores and analyze data from standardized tests to make more
informed decisions about curriculum and instruction.

   •   Throughout the district many educators are involved in the analysis of
       standardized test scores when making decisions about instruction.
   •   The data analysis is presented to parents in a number of venues, including Board
       presentations, PTA meeting, and mailings to parents).
   •   Teachers use TONYSS as a basis for placement.

Results:

Some improveme nt in student learning related (in part) to the use and analysis of
standardized tests is suggested by data from norm-referenced and criterion-referenced
tests and other forms of assessment.

   •   At Dows Lane School and Main Street School, results from K-3 performance on
       standardized tests was linked to instruction. The professionals in the building
       share this vision.

Recommendations for growth:

   •   Consider a template to standardize the implementation of instructional strategies
       on results on standardized tests.

           Longitudinal Progress of Student Performance From K through 12
                           Student Performance - Indicator #3


Long-range data measures academic progress on standardized tests and performance
assessments of the same students over the course of their attendance in the district from
grades K -12. Longitudinal data is used to make more informed decisions on improving
student performance.

Approach:

There is the beginning of a process for tracking individual student progress through
standardized test scores and performance assessments and disseminating the data to
school building leaders.

   •   The district has an approach in place to collect and analyze formal data related to
       state and local assessments K-12 and informal data related to classroom
       instruction.
   •   The district plans to have data impact classroom instruction and curriculum.

Implementation:

Some educators review data measuring the academic progress of students over time when
making decisions about curriculum and instruction.

   •   The analysis of student performance data is used to impact classroom instruction
       and, on the high school level, determines placement.
   •   The district gathers and analyzes data involving state assessments in grades 4-12.
   •   Teachers in grades K-8 collect individual informal data from multiple sources.

Results:

Some improvement in student learning related (in part) to the use of longitudinal data is
suggested by classroom and district data from multiple sources.

   •   A comparison of Irvington High School to surrounding similar districts in the area
       was included in the documentary evidence.

Areas of Strength:

   •   The district has an organized approach for the gathering of data. The focus on
       data is part of the district’s culture.

               Students as Active Participants in the Learning Process
                          Student Performance - Indicator #4


Teachers and administrators encourage and support the active participation of students in the
learning process. Educators value, measure, and use feedback from students regarding their
learning needs, an assessment of their own work and that of their peers, and their overall educational
experience to help students excel academically.

Approach:

There is a comprehensive process in place that supports and promotes the active and
informed involvement of students in their own learning through self and peer evaluation
of academic work and student fe edback. The process is revisited and revised
periodically.

   •   The district has writing rubrics in place, K-12, that outline expectations for
       student performance by skill levels. Some rubrics were developed with a
       consultant from Teachers College.
   •   The district also funded a Teachers College consultant to work with elementary
       teachers on developing their reading and writing pacing calendars. These
       calendars outline the specific skills and strategies to be addressed each month.
   •   The district has a research protocol plan in place that requires students to take
       responsibility for understanding and carrying out the research process.
   •   The district culture supports embedded reflexive thinking and student
       metacognition.

Implementation:

Many teachers regularly provide their students with opportunities to reflect on their work
and that of their peers in relation to their own expectations and those of the teacher.
Their discourse with teachers and peers about their work and their educational
experiences is extensive, thoughtful, and used to improve the instructional process.

   •   According to the district’s plan for research, students in grades 6-12 choose topics
       and develop thesis statements.
   •   Students in grades K-8 are taught to choose “just right” books for themselves
       during independent reading. This instruction helps to put the responsibility of
       choosing appropriate books on the students.
   •   Some students in grades K-3 choose literary centers during reading time and
       reflect on their center workwhich centers they are most proud of and why they
       chose those centers.
   •   At the end of some reading periods in grades K-3, teachers involve their students
       in a share during which reflections on their reading from the day and plans for
       reading the next day are discussed.
   •   Written reflections, editing and revising checklists and peer conferencing are used
       as part of the students’ writing process.

   •   Students at all levels are allowed to choose topics for research.
   •   Students in high school English classes are allowed to choose points of view to
       defend or refute.
   •   Students are offered opportunities to peer edit their written work.
   •   Teachers conference one-on-one with students to discuss their progress in reading
       and writing.
   •   Some members of the school community use ThinkWave to provide students,
       teachers and parents the ability to communicate and share information about
       curriculum, assignments and grades online.

Results:

The active and growing involvement of students in their own learning and that of their
peers is recognized as contributing to measurable improvement in their performance.

   •   In an elementary reading class, students recorded reflections on their reading
       throughout the school year. From the beginning of the year to end, it was evident
       that the student’s metacognitive statements showed growth.
   •   In high school English classes, students assessed their own ability to work in
       cooperative groups and give effective presentations. Based upon these self-
       assessments, students’ performance improved over time.
   •   Middle school students stated that, upon re-reading their writing notebook entries
       from the beginning of the year, they were able to see clear evidence of personal
       growth.

Areas of Strength:

   •   The introduction of a research protocol strengthens the overall ability of the E/LA
       program to encourage the active participation of students in their learning.
   •   The district offers numerous, authentic opportunities for students to work with
       peers and reflect on their own learning.

Innovative practices:

   •   Second grade students keep a weekly journal recording what they’ve learned in
       school each week. These written reflections are evidence of their growth in
       writing, as well as their understanding of the ir own learning.
   •   Some students are involved in their parent-teacher conferences and complete a
       conference form with their parents prior to the meeting.
   •   The Shakespeare- in-residence program engages students as actors and interpreters
       of Shakespearean drama.

                  College Enrollment and Postgraduate Experience
                          Student Performance - Indicator #5


A high percentage of students who begin their 12th year in the district graduate and go on
to attend and successfully perform in a two- or four-year college. Data about the
percentage accepted into schools of various levels of competitiveness (as defined in
Barron’s Profiles of American Colleges) is gathered and analyzed. Feedback from all
graduates regarding their preparation and success is solicited and studied.

Approach:

There is the beginning of a process to collect data about college acceptances and the
performance of graduates in their first year of college or career.

   •   The district has a beginning plan to track SAT/AP/Regents scores and students
       college destination after High School.
   •   Some college students returned and offered a workshop to high school seniors.
   •   The district’s counseling department offers many opportunities for parents and
       students to learn about colleges.
   •   The process to gather longitudinal data through the guidance department about
       student progress post graduation is in its beginning stages.


Implementation:

Some educators, including classroom teachers and counselors, receive and review data
regarding acceptance to and success in college and career to inform curriculum,
instruction and assessment decisions. Data regarding acceptance to and success in college
and career sometimes helps educators better understand and respond to changing student
needs with targeted programs.

   •   Counselors survey alumni as to their progress in college.
   •   Counselors use and disaggregate data on college acceptances.
   •   There are high school courses taught at a college level.
   •   The high school has recently Naviance software to track graduates attending
       colleges via e- mail.

Results:

Some improvement in student learning related (in part) to these programs is suggested by
classroom and district data from multiple sources.

   • More students have the opportunity to enroll in AP level courses at the high
       school. This decision suggests that student learning will increase based on the
       rigor of AP offerings.

                         Equity and the Opportunity to Learn
                               Student Performance - Indicator # 6


High quality curriculum and instruction are available to all students regardless of gender, race,

   •   The evidence of communication with the community includes budget
       presentations and mailings.

Implementation:

Many educational programs requested by teachers and administrators are valued and fully
supported by the Board and the community. Support from the Board and the community
often enables the district to meet changing student needs by providing a range of
educational programs.

   •   The budget process begins at the building level with needs articulated through
       significant staff input.
   •   The faculty is encouraged to propose programs that will directly benefit student
       performance. For example, high school English teachers are proposing expanding
       the elective program.
   •   The community has supported the budget by approving the proposed allocations
       on the first vote for the last eight years.
   •   The community supported the bond to support construction of new school
       facilities.

Results:

Measurable improvement in student learning related (in part) to programs supported by
the district budget is suggested by classroom and district data from multiple sources.

   •   Student performance scores remain high and in some cases actually improve. An
       example is the increased AP offerings at the High School, which has placed
       Irvington High School among the top performing high schools in the nation in the
       Newsweek evaluation.

Areas of Strength:

   •   The district meticulously allocates appropriate funds throughout the buildings to
       support appropriate programs.

Recommendations for growth:

   •   Consider collecting input from a greater number of faculty members when
       considering programs with budgetary implications.
   •   Consider collecting additional documentation of improved learning linked to
       resource allocation.
   •   Re-visit the need for additional special education and ESL staff and expansion of
       the high school English department faculty.
